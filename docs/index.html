1 · Problem

Plans rank sites by scores. Nobody counts the people left on slow mobile.

Today
  1. Score candidate sites on many indicators (traffic, congestion, revenue).
  2. Weight them in a meeting; build the top of the list.
  3. Compare countries on one national median speed.
  4. Rarely check whether new sites lifted nearby speeds.
This project
  1. Count people whose measured speed is below a bar.
  2. Compare countries at the same population density.
  3. Measure what real new sites did to nearby speeds (phase 3).
  4. Choose a costed set of sites that lifts the most people (phase 4).
  • Who needs it: network planners, capital-budget reviewers, regulators and policy analysts.
  • What they need: a site list with gap type, area type, cost and people helped, in their own planning tools.

The measure
  • Grid: H3 hexagons of about 0.74 km², the same size at the equator and in Germany.
  • Speed per hexagon: test-weighted geometric mean of the tiles inside it.
  • Headline: share of people in tested hexagons below 10 and 25 Mbps; untested people reported, never dropped.
Test design
  • Gates first: every phase's pass mark was pushed to GitHub before any analysis (appendix).
  • Held-out areas: predictions are scored on whole areas the model never saw, sized like the real gaps between tests.
  • Traceable: 40 input files fingerprinted (SHA-256).

4 · Solution

Most people on slow mobile were never tested

How to read this
  • Shows: people whose typical speed is below the bar; untested places use a predicted speed.
  • Counting: a hexagon 60% likely below the bar counts 0.6 of its people.
  • Last tile: the pre-set measure, the raw test average, which single unlucky tests inflate.
  • So what: the first tile is who a site plan must reach; most were never tested.

    • Dot: a site point in a 0.74 km² search area, coloured by the kind of site.
    • Checks: people on both population maps (the smaller counts) and at least 1,000 m² of buildings.
    • Placement: the dot sits on the built-up pixel nearest the buildings' centre, never in a lake.
    • Dark ring: steep (over 20°) or high (over 2,500 m); access costs more.
    • Purple: existing LTE/5G cells in OpenCelliD. New sites fill the gaps; capacity sits on purple.

    Caveats
    • Tests are not a random sample: people test when it is slow or new; shares count people, not tests.
    • Untested speeds are predictions: machine learning predicts them 30–45% slower than kriging; a pre-set 2026 Q4 test decides (accuracy).
    • Not a licence check: regulators test obligations with their own measurements, not crowdsourced averages.
    • Population maps disagree where data is thin: 17% in the US, 27–37% in Southeast Asia and the UAE (details).
    • "New site" means no tower on record: OpenCelliD misses most Southeast Asian towers; check against an operator's site list.
    • Gap types only where cell data allows: mainly city areas in Germany, France, the US and the UAE (details).
    FAQ
    Is Germany really behind Indonesia?
    • No. As measured they look close: 14.6% vs 13.3% of tested people below 25 Mbps.
    • Indonesia's tested people live mostly in cities. At equal density: Indonesia 22.0%, Germany 17.7%.
    • Counting everyone, untested people included: Indonesia about 18%, Germany about 12%.
    What is "typical speed"?
    • The speed most people in a hexagon usually get; one test can be unlucky (a busy hour, indoors).
    • Each hexagon's tests are blended with its neighbourhood: 1 test borrows 75–90%; 200 tests keep their own.
    • Checked on the next quarter: blended speeds are 28–44% closer than raw ones.
    Why 10 and 25 Mbps?
    • Both were fixed before the analysis; 5, 50 and 100 Mbps are shown as sensitivity.
    • 100 Mbps matches Germany's 2019 licence obligation and the IMT-2020 user-experienced rate.
    Where should the next site go?
    • In each plan's 0.74 km² search areas: dots on the map; site lists under the plan chart.
    • Each is ranked by people lifted per cost and tagged macro, small cell or extra capacity.
    • All have people on both maps and buildings on the ground; a planner picks the rooftop or tower.
    Why two population maps?
    • Each map can be wrong in remote places: Kontur spreads people over land; WorldPop only into detected settlements.
    • Example: an original Indonesian nominal had 28,665 people in Kontur and none in WorldPop.
    • Counting the smaller keeps plans where both maps agree people live.
    How often can it refresh?
    • Quarterly, after each Ookla release; every refresh fingerprints all 40 inputs.
    • A scheduled Databricks job runs the pipeline for Indonesia straight from Ookla's source files.
    5 · Our take

    The gap is rural, local, and partly unseen

    • Rural first: rural people are about 3–6× as likely as city-centre people to be below 25 Mbps (Malaysia far more).
    • Untested places hold most of the gap: 13–23M Filipinos and 22–30M Indonesians nobody tested are likely below 25 Mbps.
    • Single tests mislead: removing test noise shrinks slow places in the US, France and the UAE, not in Southeast Asia.
    • New sites lift some slow places: up to 1 in 8 crossed 25 Mbps; average speed rose only in the US.
    • Count people, not scores: checked plans lift 1.5–3.8× as many people as KPI ranking; unchecked, it looked up to 9×.
    • What would change our view: the pre-set 2026 Q4 test, local cost data, or a third population map.

    How to read this
    • Error: measured on a log scale; the typical-miss column turns it into everyday terms.
    • Example: a 3.5× typical miss means a 20 Mbps prediction could be 6–70 Mbps.
    • Ratio: one model's error divided by kriging's; 0.93 means 7% smaller.
    • So what: treat single-hexagon predictions as chances; trust district and country totals more.
    How to read this
    • One test varies by: how much two tests at the same place differ.
    • Real spots differ by: how much typical speed truly varies inside one 0.74 km² hexagon.
    • So what: a hexagon with 1–2 tests is mostly luck; the neighbourhood tells you more.
    How to read this
    • Test: Q2 speeds blended with their neighbourhood, then compared with Q3's separate tests.
    • Closer by: how much smaller the error became.
    • Level check: do blended speeds reproduce the share below 25 Mbps seen in Q3?
    • So what: blending helps everywhere; corrected shares are credible (✓) in four countries, provisional (⚠) in three.
    • 80% ranges hold 84% of the time: a range like 9–45 Mbps contained the measured value 84 times in 100.
    • Ranking slow vs fast (AUC 0.76): picks the slower of two hexagons 76% of the time; a coin flip scores 50%.
    • Inside the model's comfort zone (95%): 95% of untested people live where inputs resemble the training places.
    • Coverage vs capacity from Ookla alone (AUC 0.51): speed patterns cannot tell the gap types apart, so the idea was dropped.

    • Average speed: change in mean download after a new site, vs similar places without one yet.

    Technical appendix · glossary, gates, intervals, assignment checks
    Hexagon (0.74 km²)
    The analysis grid: equal-size cells (H3 resolution 8), about 0.9 km across.
    Tested hexagon
    A hexagon with at least one Ookla speed test this quarter. 61% of people live in one.
    Share below the bar
    People in tested hexagons whose average download is below e.g. 25 Mbps, divided by all people in tested hexagons.
    95% interval
    A range that very likely contains the true value. Example: 12.9–21.0% means "somewhere in there, most likely near 16%".
    At equal density
    Each country re-weighted to the same mix of dense and sparse areas, so archipelagos and dense cities compare fairly.
    Slow cluster (FDR 5%)
    Neighbouring slow hexagons unlikely to be chance; at most 5% of flagged clusters are false alarms.
    Typical speed
    What most people in a hexagon usually get, after blending its few tests with its neighbourhood (empirical Bayes).
    Error (log scale) / typical miss
    How far predictions are from measurements; 1.24 on the log scale means a typical miss of about 3.5×.
    Ratio vs kriging
    One model's error divided by kriging's. 0.93 = 7% smaller error. The pre-set rule needed 0.90 or lower.
    Kriging
    The standard way to estimate a value at a place from nearby measurements, weighting closer and more similar ones more.
    LightGBM
    A machine-learning model (gradient-boosted trees) that also uses population, places, cells, home-broadband speed and history.
    Spatial block test
    Hide whole ~36 km² areas and predict them, like the real distance from untested people to a test.
    AUC
    How well a score ranks slow above fast places: 0.5 = coin flip, 1.0 = perfect.
    80% range coverage
    How often stated 80% ranges contain the measured value; 84% = honest, slightly cautious.
    Area of applicability
    Where a prediction's inputs resemble the places the model learned from; outside it, predictions are flagged.
    New-tower equivalent
    The cost unit: new macro site 1, small cell 0.5, extra capacity 0.3 (ratios from a US regulator report).
    Expected people lifted
    People × chance their typical speed is below 25 Mbps × the lift rate measured for that kind of site.
    Two population maps
    Kontur and WorldPop estimate where people live by different methods; plans count the smaller number per hexagon.
    Built-up check
    At least 1,000 m² of buildings in the hexagon (ESA WorldCover, 10 m), about seven village houses.
    Site point
    The built-up pixel nearest the centre of the hexagon's buildings: where a planner starts looking.
    Steep / high
    Slope over 20° or elevation over 2,500 m at the site point; flagged, not rejected.
    ✓ credible / ⚠ provisional
    Whether the noise-corrected country share passed the cross-quarter level check (within 1.5 points).
