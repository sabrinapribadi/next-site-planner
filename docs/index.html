1 · Problem

Plans rank sites by scores. Nobody counts the people left on slow mobile.

Today
  1. Score candidate sites on many indicators (traffic, congestion, revenue).
  2. Weight them in a meeting; build the top of the list.
  3. Compare countries on one national median speed.
  4. Rarely check whether new sites lifted nearby speeds.
This project
  1. Count people whose measured speed is below a bar.
  2. Compare countries at the same population density.
  3. Measure what real new sites did to nearby speeds (phase 3).
  4. Choose a costed set of sites that lifts the most people (phase 4).
  • Who needs it: network planners, capital-budget reviewers, regulators and policy analysts.
  • What they need: a site list with gap type, area type, cost and people helped, in their own planning tools.

The measure
  • Grid: H3 hexagons of about 0.74 km², the same size at the equator and in Germany.
  • Speed per hexagon: test-weighted geometric mean of the tiles inside it.
  • Headline: share of people in tested hexagons below 10 and 25 Mbps; untested people reported, never dropped.
Test design
  • Gates first: every phase's pass mark was pushed to GitHub before any analysis (appendix).
  • Held-out areas: predictions are scored on whole areas the model never saw, sized like the real gaps between tests.
  • Traceable: 40 input files fingerprinted (SHA-256).

4 · Solution

Most people on slow mobile were never tested

How to read this

"Typically get" uses each hexagon's typical speed (see the FAQ "What is typical speed?") or, where nobody tested, a prediction; a hexagon with a 60% chance of being below the bar counts 0.6 of its people. The last tile is the measure fixed before the analysis: the raw average of whatever tests were run, which single unlucky tests inflate. So what: the first tile is the best estimate of how many people a site plan should reach; most of them are invisible in test statistics.

    How to read this

    Red dot: share of tested people whose measured average is below the bar; the pale line is its 95% interval (the true share very likely lies inside). Hollow circle: the same share if every country had the same mix of dense and sparse areas (10 and 25 Mbps). Amber square: everyone, using typical speeds and including people nobody tested.     Caveats
    • Tests are not a random sample: people test when it is slow or new; shares count people, not tests.
    • Untested speeds are predictions, and may be too optimistic: the deployed method (kriging) borrows from nearby tested places; a machine-learning model predicts untested places 30–45% slower. A test on the next quarter's data, fixed in advance, will decide (accuracy).
    • Not a licence check: regulators test obligations with their own measurements, not crowdsourced averages.
    • Gap types only where cell data allows: coverage vs capacity is known mainly for city areas in Germany, France, the US and the UAE (details).
    FAQ
    Is Germany really behind Indonesia?

    No. As measured they look close (14.6% vs 13.3% below 25 Mbps), but Indonesia's tested people live mostly in cities. At equal density Indonesia is at 22.0% and Germany at 17.7%. Counting everyone, including people nobody tested, Indonesia is at about 18–20% and Germany at about 11–16%.

    What is "typical speed"?

    The speed most people in a hexagon usually get. One test can be unlucky (a busy hour, a cheap plan, indoors). We blend each hexagon's tests with its neighbourhood: a hexagon with 1 test takes about 75–90% from its neighbours, one with 200 tests keeps its own. Checked against the next quarter's tests: 28–44% closer than the raw value.

    Why 10 and 25 Mbps?

    Both were fixed before the analysis. 5, 50 and 100 Mbps are shown as sensitivity; 100 Mbps matches Germany's 2019 licence obligation and the IMT-2020 user-experienced rate.

    Where should the next site go?

    Not yet answered. The list shows where most people are below the bar today. Phase 2 predicts untested areas, phase 3 measures what new sites do, phase 4 chooses a costed set.

    How often can it refresh?

    Quarterly, after each Ookla release. Every refresh records a fingerprint of all 40 inputs.

    5 · Our take

    The gap is rural, local, and partly unseen

    • Rural first: in most countries rural people are 3 to 6 times more likely than city-centre people to be below 25 Mbps.
    • Untested places hold most of the gap: about 23M Filipinos and 30M Indonesians nobody tested are likely below 25 Mbps — more than among those who did test.
    • Single tests mislead: once test noise is removed, the US, France and the UAE have far fewer slow places than raw numbers suggest; Indonesia and the Philippines do not.
    • Local, not regional: real slow clusters exist only in Indonesia and the Philippines; elsewhere slow places are scattered, so plans must target places, not regions.
    • What would change our view: new sites found not to lift nearby speeds (phase 3), or the next quarter's tests disagreeing with the predictions.

    How to read this

    For people below 25 Mbps: a coverage gap has no LTE/5G cell recorded within about 1.3 km (needs a new site); a capacity gap has cells nearby but is still slow (needs more capacity on existing sites). "Type unknown" is used where the public cell database is too incomplete to tell. Example: Germany — 5.5% of people are in capacity gaps, 1.1% in coverage gaps. So what: where the type is known (mostly city areas in Germany, France, the US and the UAE), slow areas need capacity far more often than new coverage; in Southeast Asia the type cannot be told from public data.

    Slow clusters (FDR 5%)
    How to read this

    "Clusters" counts 1 km hexagons that sit in a group of slow neighbours beyond what chance explains. "False on shuffled" re-runs the test after randomly shuffling speeds, so any cluster found is a false alarm; the old method's count is in brackets. So what: zero false alarms means the clusters shown are real; slow places elsewhere are scattered, not clustered.

    Speed where nobody tested · test noise · how good the predictions are
    How to read this

    The error is measured on a log scale; the "typical miss" column turns it into everyday terms. Example: a typical miss of 3.5× means a prediction of 20 Mbps could really be about 6–70 Mbps. The ratio compares two errors: 0.93 = machine learning's error is 93% of kriging's, i.e. 7% smaller. So what: single-hexagon predictions are rough; use them as chances ("70% likely below 25 Mbps") and trust district and country totals more.

    How to read this

    "One test varies by" = how much two tests at the same place differ. "Real spots differ by" = how much the typical speed truly differs between spots inside one 0.74 km² hexagon. So what: one test tells you far less than the neighbourhood does; a hexagon with 1–2 tests is mostly luck.

    How to read this

    We blended 2026 Q2 speeds with their neighbourhood, then compared with Q3's separate tests. "Closer by" = how much smaller the error became. The level check adds Q3's test noise back and asks whether the blended speeds reproduce the share below 25 Mbps actually seen. So what: blending works for every country; the corrected country shares are credible (✓) in four countries and provisional (⚠) in three.

    • 80% ranges hold 84% of the time: a stated range like "9–45 Mbps" contained the measured value in 84 of 100 cases — the uncertainty is honest, slightly cautious.
    • Ranking slow vs fast (AUC 0.76): given one truly slow and one fast hexagon, the model ranks the slow one as more likely slow 76% of the time (50% = coin flip). Good for prioritising, not for verdicts.
    • Inside the model's comfort zone (95%): 95% of untested people live where the inputs look like places the model learned from.
    • Coverage vs capacity from Ookla alone (AUC 0.51): upload/download ratio and latency cannot tell the two gap types apart — a coin flip, so that idea was dropped.
    Decision log · every choice against a named alternative
    • ADR-001 — people below a bar on H3 resolution 8; raw and density-adjusted; untested people reported.
    • ADR-002 — gates for every phase, set before analysis; two logged deviations (block bootstrap, exact p-values).
    • ADR-003 — how planners work: gap types, Degree of Urbanisation, 3GPP site spacing, co-location costs, regulator benchmarks; OpenCelliD completeness finding.
    • ADR-004 — speed where nobody tested: kriging vs LightGBM, test blocks sized to real distances; the gate failed and kriging is used.
    • ADR-005 — test noise (written after the gate failed, before running): noise model, blending with the neighbourhood, cross-quarter check.

    The three method changes made after checks failed are in the method table above and ADR-002; the noise follow-up is ADR-005.

    Technical appendix · glossary, gates, intervals, assignment checks
    Hexagon (0.74 km²)
    The analysis grid: equal-size cells (H3 resolution 8), about 0.9 km across.
    Tested hexagon
    A hexagon with at least one Ookla speed test this quarter. 61% of people live in one.
    Share below the bar
    People in tested hexagons whose average download is below e.g. 25 Mbps, divided by all people in tested hexagons.
    95% interval
    A range that very likely contains the true value. Example: 12.9–21.0% means "somewhere in there, most likely near 16%".
    At equal density
    Each country re-weighted to the same mix of dense and sparse areas, so archipelagos and dense cities compare fairly.
    Slow cluster (FDR 5%)
    A group of neighbouring slow hexagons unlikely to be chance; at most 5% of flagged clusters are expected to be false alarms.
    Typical speed
    What most people in a hexagon usually get, after blending its few tests with its neighbourhood (empirical Bayes).
    Error (log scale) / typical miss
    How far predictions are from measurements. An error of 1.24 means a typical miss of about 3.5× (e^1.24): 20 Mbps predicted, 6–70 Mbps possible.
    Ratio vs kriging
    One model's error divided by kriging's. 0.93 = 7% smaller error. The pre-set rule needed 0.90 or lower.
    Kriging
    The standard way to estimate a value at a place from nearby measurements, weighting closer and more similar ones more.
    LightGBM
    A machine-learning model (gradient-boosted trees) that also uses population, places, cells, home-broadband speed and history.
    Spatial block test
    Hold out whole areas (~36 km²) and predict them from the rest, mimicking how far untested people live from a test.
    AUC
    How well a score ranks slow above fast places: 0.5 = coin flip, 1.0 = perfect.
    80% range coverage
    How often stated 80% ranges contain the measured value; 84% = honest, slightly cautious.
    Area of applicability
    Where a prediction's inputs resemble the places the model learned from; outside it, predictions are flagged.
    ✓ credible / ⚠ provisional
    Whether the noise-corrected country share passed the cross-quarter level check (within 1.5 points).

    How to read this

    Two ways of computing the 95% interval for the share below 25 Mbps. The pre-registered one (resampling speed tiles) is biased: ✕ marks where its interval does not even contain the measured share. The one used resamples ~250 km² blocks. So what: intervals on this page come from the block method; the failed method is kept for transparency.

    How to read this

    "Assigned" compares people and tests placed on the grid with a recount against detailed borders; above 100% means a few coastal hexagons whose centre lies just offshore were counted (≤ 0.3% sit in a neighbouring country). "Kontur ÷ World Bank" compares the population layer with official totals; France and the US exclude overseas areas and Alaska/Hawaii, and Kontur under-counts the UAE's migrant workforce. So what: no country loses people or tests; population totals are close to official ones.
